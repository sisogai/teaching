3.1 Production Technology

Producers use inputs such as labor, capital, land, and intermediate goods to create outputs. A production technology describes which outputs can be produced from which input combinations.

A more efficient technology can produce more output from the same inputs, or the same output from fewer inputs. This is why technology and cost structure are closely connected.

Definition 3.1 — Production Technology

A production technology is a way to transform inputs into outputs. The technology determines the producer's cost structure.

3.2 Cost Function

A cost function gives the minimum amount of money needed to produce a given quantity of output. In this page, the baseline example is

$$ C(Q)=aQ^2+bQ+FC \quad (Q>0), \qquad C(0)=0. $$
Definition 3.2.1 — Fixed, Variable, and Total Cost

Fixed cost is incurred whenever the firm produces a positive amount. Variable cost changes with output. Total cost is the sum of fixed and variable costs.

Definition 3.2.3 — Marginal and Average Cost
$$ MC(Q)=\frac{dC}{dQ}(Q), \qquad AC(Q)=\frac{C(Q)}{Q}. $$

Marginal cost is useful for deciding whether output should be adjusted. Average cost is useful for deciding whether profit is positive.

3.3 Profit Maximization

The firm chooses output to maximize revenue minus cost:

$$ \max_{Q\geq 0} \; pQ-C(Q). $$
Proposition 3.3.1 — First-Order Condition

If \(Q^*>0\) attains maximum profit, then

$$ p=\frac{dC}{dQ}(Q^*)=MC(Q^*). $$

Definition 3.3.3 — Supply Function (Long Run)

Suppose the production horizon is the long run, i.e., \(FC = 0\). The supply function \(s(p)\) gives the profit-maximizing output for each price \(p\):

$$ s(p) = \arg\max_{Q\geq 0}\; pQ - C(Q). $$

The maximized profit, expressed as a function of \(p\), is called the profit function:

$$ \pi(p) = \max_{Q\geq 0}\; pQ - C(Q) = ps(p) - C(s(p)). $$

3.4 Profit and Supply in the Short Run

With fixed costs, the firm may choose not to produce. The marginal-cost condition gives the best positive production level, but the firm still compares producing with \(Q=0\).

Definition 3.4.2 — Short-Run Supply

The shutdown price is the price at which \(p=AVC(Q)\). The break-even price is the price at which \(p=AC(Q)\). Below the shutdown price, the firm supplies zero.

3.5 Two Production Case

Consider a firm producing two goods \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) with prices \(p_1, p_2\) and joint cost \(C(Q_1,Q_2)\). The profit maximization problem is

$$ \max_{Q_1,Q_2}\; p_1 Q_1 + p_2 Q_2 - C(Q_1, Q_2). $$
Proposition 3.5.1 — First-Order Conditions (Two Goods)

If \((Q_1^*, Q_2^*)\) with \(Q_i^* > 0\) attains maximum profit, then

$$ [Q_1]\quad p_1 = \frac{\partial C}{\partial Q_1}(Q_1^*, Q_2^*), \qquad [Q_2]\quad p_2 = \frac{\partial C}{\partial Q_2}(Q_1^*, Q_2^*). $$

Here \(\partial C/\partial Q_i\) is the partial derivative — the derivative with respect to \(Q_i\) holding the other good fixed. Geometrically, at the maximum the profit surface \(\pi(Q_1,Q_2)\) has a flat tangent plane: both partial derivatives vanish simultaneously (drag the graph below to see this).

3.6 Deriving Cost Functions

Cost functions can be derived from a cost-minimization problem. With labor \(L\), capital \(K\), wage \(w\), and rental rate \(r\), the firm solves

$$ \min_{L,K} \; wL+rK \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(L,K)\geq \bar Q. $$
Tangency Condition
$$ \frac{w}{r}=MRT=\frac{f_L(L,K)}{f_K(L,K)}. $$

3.8 Aggregation

In an economy with many firms, market supply is the sum of individual supplies at each price:

$$ S(p)=s_1(p)+s_2(p)+\cdots. $$

Since each individual supply is upward sloping in the standard case, the market supply curve is also upward sloping.
