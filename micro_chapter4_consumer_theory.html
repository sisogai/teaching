4.1 Consumption Bundles and Budget Constraints

With $L$ goods, a consumer's spending pattern is described by a consumption bundle $x = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_L)$. Given prices $p = (p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_L)$ and income $w > 0$, the set of bundles the consumer can afford is determined by the following condition.

Definition 4.1.1 — Budget Constraint

A consumption bundle $x$ is feasible (affordable) if it satisfies the budget constraint:

$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 + \cdots + p_L x_L \leq w \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad p \cdot x \leq w$$

The set of all feasible bundles is the budget set; its upper-right boundary is the budget line.

In the two-good case the budget line can be written as:

$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = w \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad x_2 = -\frac{p_1}{p_2}\,x_1 + \frac{w}{p_2}$$

4.2 Consumers' Actual Choice Behaviors

A key principle in economics is the revealed-preference approach: rather than asking consumers what they want, we infer their preferences from what they actually choose. What a consumer could have bought but did not tells us a great deal about their tastes.

Definition 4.2.2 — Revealed Preference

Consider a consumer who chooses bundle $x$ from a feasible set that also contains $y$. Then $x$ is revealed preferred to $y$, written $x \succ y$.

Two regularity conditions are imposed: (1) consumers always make some choice, and (2) preferences are stable — if $x \succ y$ is revealed in one situation, the same ranking holds in all other situations.

An important implication of revealed preference is that a lump-sum tax is welfare-superior to a commodity tax that raises the same revenue. A commodity tax on good 1 (raising its price from $p_1$ to $p_1+t$) rotates the budget line inward; the consumer's chosen bundle $x'$ under the commodity tax is still affordable under a lump-sum tax of $tx'_1$ at the original prices. Hence the consumer can do at least as well under the lump-sum tax.

4.3 Preference and Utility Function

From observed choice behaviors we can infer a ranking among consumption bundles. Working directly with preference relations, however, is awkward. Instead, economists represent preferences by a utility function, which allows the same mathematical techniques used in producer theory.

Definition 4.3.1 — Utility Function

A utility function $u(x)$ assigns a real number to each consumption bundle so as to represent the preference ordering:

$$x \succ y \iff u(x) > u(y)$$

Note: only the ordering of utility values matters, not their absolute levels. Any positive monotone transformation of $u$ represents the same preferences (ordinal utility).

Definition 4.3.4 — Indifference Curve

Given utility level $\bar u$, the indifference curve is the set of all bundles that yield exactly that utility: $$I(\bar u) = \{x : u(x) = \bar u\}.$$ Indifference curves are the consumer analogue of isoquants in producer theory. Bundles on a higher indifference curve are preferred.

Common examples of utility functions:

$$u(x_1, x_2) = x_1^\alpha\, x_2^{1-\alpha}, \quad \alpha \in (0,1)$$

Indifference curves are convex toward the origin. Larger $\alpha$ reflects a stronger preference for good 1.

$$u(x_1, x_2) = ax_1 + bx_2, \quad a, b > 0$$

Indifference curves are straight lines (perfect substitutes). The MRS is constant.

$$u(x_1, x_2) = \min\{ax_1,\, bx_2\}, \quad a, b > 0$$

Indifference curves are L-shaped (perfect complements). The optimal bundle always satisfies $ax_1 = bx_2$.

4.4 Utility-Maximization Problem
Definition 4.4.1 — Utility-Maximization Problem (UMP)

Given utility function $u(x_1,x_2)$ and budget constraint, the consumer solves: $$\max_{x_1,x_2}\; u(x_1,x_2) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \leq w.$$ The solutions $x_1(p_1,p_2,w)$ and $x_2(p_1,p_2,w)$ are the Walrasian (Marshallian) demand functions. The value of utility at the optimum is the indirect utility function: $$v(p_1,p_2,w) = \max_{p_1 x_1+p_2 x_2 \leq w} u(x_1,x_2) = u\bigl(x_1(p_1,p_2,w),\, x_2(p_1,p_2,w)\bigr).$$

Cobb–Douglas case: $u(x_1,x_2) = x_1^\alpha x_2^{1-\alpha}$. The Lagrangian first-order condition gives the optimality condition MRS = price ratio:

$$\frac{u_1}{u_2} = \frac{p_1}{p_2} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{x_2}{x_1} = \frac{p_1}{p_2}$$
Walrasian Demand & Indirect Utility (Cobb–Douglas, Example 4.4.2)
$$x_1^* = \frac{\alpha\, w}{p_1}, \qquad x_2^* = \frac{(1-\alpha)\, w}{p_2}$$

The consumer spends fraction $\alpha$ of income on good 1 and $(1-\alpha)$ on good 2 — the constant expenditure-share rule.

The indirect utility function is:

$$v(p_1,p_2,w) = \left(\frac{\alpha}{p_1}\right)^{\!\alpha} \left(\frac{1-\alpha}{p_2}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} w$$

$v$ is decreasing in prices, increasing in wealth — richer consumers or lower prices always raise utility.

Show derivation

From the first-order conditions:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_1} = \lambda p_1 \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha x_1^{\alpha-1}x_2^{1-\alpha} = \lambda p_1$$ $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_2} = \lambda p_2 \;\Rightarrow\; (1-\alpha)x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{-\alpha} = \lambda p_2$$

Dividing the two equations gives:

$$\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{x_2}{x_1} = \frac{p_1}{p_2} \;\Rightarrow\; x_2 = \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{p_1}{p_2}x_1$$

Substituting into the budget constraint $p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = w$:

$$p_1 x_1 + \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}p_1 x_1 = w \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{p_1 x_1}{\alpha} = w \;\Rightarrow\; x_1^* = \frac{\alpha w}{p_1}$$

4.5 Expenditure-Minimization Problem
Definition 4.5.1 — Expenditure-Minimization Problem (EMP)

Given prices and a target utility level $\bar u$, the consumer solves: $$\min_{x_1,x_2}\; p_1x_1+p_2x_2 \quad \text{s.t.}\quad u(x_1,x_2)\geq \bar u.$$ The solution is the Hicksian demand $h(p_1,p_2,\bar u)$, and the minimized cost is the expenditure function $e(p_1,p_2,\bar u)$.

Cobb-Douglas case: for $u(x_1,x_2)=x_1^\alpha x_2^{1-\alpha}$, the cheapest bundle reaches the target indifference curve exactly and has an isocost line tangent to it:

$$h_1=\bar u\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha},\qquad h_2=\bar u\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)^\alpha.$$

The expenditure function gives the minimum wealth needed to reach $\bar u$ at the given prices.

4.6 Comparative Analysis of Demand Functions

How does the Walrasian demand $x_i(p_1,p_2,w)$ respond to changes in prices and income? The answers are often non-trivial because budget constraints create interactions between goods.

4.6.1 — Wealth Changes: Normal and Inferior Goods

When wealth $w$ increases (prices held fixed), the budget set expands in a parallel outward shift. If demand for good $i$ rises with wealth, it is a normal good; if it falls, it is an inferior good. Most goods are normal, but inferior goods arise when consumers substitute toward higher-quality alternatives as income grows.

For Cobb–Douglas utility, demand $x_1^* = \alpha w/p_1$ is linear in $w$, so good 1 is always a normal good with income elasticity exactly 1.

4.6.2 — Price Changes: Substitution and Wealth Effects

When $p_1$ falls, demand for good 1 changes through two channels:

  • Substitution effect: Good 1 is now cheaper relative to good 2, so the consumer substitutes toward good 1. This effect always increases demand for the cheaper good.
  • Wealth effect: A lower price raises real purchasing power. If good 1 is normal this reinforces the substitution effect; if it is inferior the two effects partially offset.

A Giffen good is one for which the wealth effect dominates, so demand falls when the price falls. This is theoretically possible but empirically rare.

To measure the size of demand responses independently of units, we use elasticities — the ratio of percentage changes in demand to percentage changes in the variable of interest.

Definition 4.6.3 — Elasticities of Demand
$$\varepsilon_w = \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial w}\cdot\frac{w}{x_i} \quad\text{(income elasticity)}$$ $$\varepsilon_{ii} = -\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial p_i}\cdot\frac{p_i}{x_i} \quad\text{(own-price elasticity)}$$ $$\varepsilon_{ij} = \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial p_j}\cdot\frac{p_j}{x_i} \quad\text{(cross-price elasticity, } j\neq i\text{)}$$

Elasticity compares percentage changes, not unit changes. The same price increase can be a small percentage change at a high price but a large percentage change at a low price. The interactive below shows this directly on a demand curve.
